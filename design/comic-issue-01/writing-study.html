Giving the characters room to speak

The opening needs conversations that develop, reveal character and change the situation. Increasing balloon length alone would leave the underlying problem intact.

Sean’s criticism is specific: nearly every panel offers an observation and a neat reply, so the characters sound as if they are supplying comic-book lines instead of dealing with a difficult job. His reference to Rising Stars asks for substance, history and thought where they belong. It does not ask us to imitate J. What changes the approach

A conversation needs an objective that survives the panel boundary. Characters want different things from the exchange. A question may produce an evasion, an unwelcome admission, a challenge or a change of tactics. Tony Cliff’s worked examples make this especially clear: information becomes dramatically useful when the speakers have reasons to offer or withhold it. Our hotel manager should be trying to preserve the wedding and his credibility through a whole scene, rather than confessing his priorities in one polished joke. Cliff’s guide

Longer speech works when it develops an idea and changes the listener’s situation. In Andy Diggle’s Green Arrow: Year One opening, a post-rescue reprimand expands from a practical dispute into a judgment about a man’s life. The response then avoids that judgment, which makes the joke revealing. That is a useful example of earned length, not a style template for Tony and Sean. Diggle’s script

Substance can also accumulate through short replies and silence. Busiek’s retirement-party scene and DeConnick’s hearing and family scenes develop attitudes across an exchange. The reader learns more with each beat. This differs from several unrelated quips occupying adjacent panels. We should preserve the silent duct-tape discovery and Sean’s requested hesitant “Uh…” while giving him more room elsewhere. Busiek · DeConnick

Text and pictures share both space and time. A deliberate explanation can occupy a still moment, a sequence of inspections, or several changing reactions. A split-second impact cannot comfortably contain the same speech. Bennett and Zub treat pacing as a relationship among panels, actions and words; Piekos makes the production consequences explicit. A denser scene must be planned before Art fills the available space. Bennett · Zub · Piekos

There is no single prescribed workflow or universal balloon word limit in these sources. Pak allows dialogue-first or panel-first drafting. Zub likes establishing voice before art. DeConnick’s introductory artist letter allows later adjustment. Some guides prefer very economical text; that preference should not override Sean’s explicit taste. We will test the actual page at its 6⅝ × 10¼-inch trim rather than treating an internet word-count rule as a substitute for judgment.

A practical method for the next pass

  1. Write the scene’s human problem before its dialogue. Identify what each speaker needs, what they know, what they conceal and what changes by the end. This can remain a few editorial sentences; it need not become another layer of world-building.
  2. Draft the whole exchange without forcing a joke into each panel. Keep a continuing subject and allow an answer to alter the next question. Let an explanation, remembered job or disagreement earn a longer speech. Any new personal history remains a proposal for Sean, not invented canon.
  3. Give the voices different habits of attention. Tony notices consequences and explains practical judgments patiently until danger requires commands. Sean can think aloud, challenge an implausible explanation and still become momentarily speechless. The manager protects his event and avoids blame. BJ hears a service call and calculates commercial benefit. These are working voice directions grounded in Sean’s feedback.
  4. Break the exchange into drawable moments. Choose where a thought continues in linked balloons, where the listener’s reaction deserves a panel and where physical work can carry time. Protect the silent tape reveal. Use Art’s judgment about composition and revise the layout when the scene needs room.
  5. Read aloud and remove the names. Check whether the sentence is something this person would choose here; then check whether the voice is distinguishable. Avoid using verbal tics, heavy dialect or constant punchlines to manufacture distinction. Read the entire conversation, not isolated quotable lines.
  6. Proof the lettering at actual size. Number every balloon, caption and sound effect. Check reading order, mouth-directed tails, radio consistency, padding and the time implied by the action. If a worthwhile speech does not fit, change the beat allocation before shrinking type.
  7. Review one complete revised scene with Sean. Show every affected page in full. Compare it with this visual draft and settle the voices before extending the issue. Keep publication approval separate.

For a longer Tony speech, the test will be whether we learn how his experience affects a decision and how Sean responds to that decision. For a longer manager speech, the test will be whether he is trying to persuade, excuse or conceal something. Length has to do work. We should also leave room for a joke that exposes a relationship or makes fear bearable; the aim is to stop making every exchange resolve in the same way.

Sources studied and their limits

Sixteen substantive guides, interview/transcript texts and professional script excerpts are annotated below. Comic read the linked craft material and opening script excerpts; Storyboarding independently checked overlapping sources and additional passages identified below. This is a focused craft study, not a claim to have read every available guide or every complete comic. Search snippets and inaccessible resources are not counted.

1. Anina Bennett — Writing for Comics

Her guide connects visual thinking, breakdowns, panel time and revision. Conversation can use repeated compositions, changing reactions or different shot sizes. Words should contribute more than a description of visible action.

Read: relevant visual-thinking, pacing, breakdown and dialogue sections of the web guide. Storyboarding also read corresponding sections of the 2006 PDF. Older market advice was excluded.

2. Jim Zub — How I Write Comics, Part Two

Causal preparation and character motivation make a plot’s developments feel earned. For this opening, the wedding, maintenance failures and manager’s concealment should generate the emergency through connected choices.

Read: main article.

3. Jim Zub — Part Three

A page-by-page breakdown distributes emphasis and reveals pacing problems before full scripting. For us, the monster reveal and room for conversation should be considered together. Check page turns in the actual assembled book; a rule about odd/even pages without cover placement is insufficient.

Read: main article.

4. Jim Zub — Part Four

Panel descriptions identify the essential story information, while dialogue gives the artist a character’s voice. Numbered lettering and clear acting intentions help the team interpret the same scene.

Read: article text; embedded script-page images were not used as a separate example.

5. Jim Zub — Part Five

Dialogue depends on attitude, purpose and cadence; reading aloud exposes generic phrasing. His warning about long speeches during instantaneous action matters more here than adopting his personal preference for brevity everywhere.

Read: main dialogue guide.

6. Greg Pak — How I Write a Comic Book Script

Pak describes flexible paths through outline, panels and dialogue, with attention to emotional turns and revision. There is room to discover a conversation before settling its final panel structure.

Read: main craft article.

7. Nate Piekos — Comic Script Basics

Separate production directions from printed text and number lettering elements clearly. Sentence-case scripting and deliberate emphasis instructions make intention easier to distinguish from routine presentation.

Read: script-format guide.

8. Nate Piekos — Comic Book Grammar & Tradition

Balloon joins, tails, emphasis and broadcast treatment communicate delivery and sequence. A consistent BJ radio style should remain distinguishable from the manager’s shouted speech. These conventions permit editorial variation.

Read: relevant balloon, broadcast, emphasis and quiet-speech sections; not treated as a universal word-count rule.

9. Writing Excuses 7.44 — Writing for Comics with Jim Zub

The discussion of retaining conversational links while condensing a scene directly addresses our stop-start rhythm. A trimmed exchange still needs to feel as though one person has heard the other.

Read: transcript sections on scripting, collaboration, compression and page turns; audio was not listened to.

10. Palle Schmidt — Five Tips on Writing Dialogue

Different wants, indirect answers and varied rhythms help an exchange feel inhabited. The advice to keep dialogue short is useful for removing waste, but we will not turn it into a prohibition against the fuller speech Sean wants.

Read: complete main article.

11. Tony Cliff — How to Write Better Dialogue

The worked revisions distinguish information delivery, meandering realism and purposeful disagreement. Cliff also notes that many alternating balloons have a spatial cost; one developed statement can sometimes be clearer than several tiny exchanges.

Read: full article text, including contributions attributed there to Jordan Morris, Dik Pose and Ben Hatke.

12. P. J. Holden — Creating Comics 2: Writing a Script

The artist needs essential geography and story facts before drawing. Shot choice, panel count and balloon room interact; a large reveal reduces room elsewhere. Drawn layouts are a practical test of the script.

Read: craft section; submission-market statements are outside this study.

13. Kieron Gillen and Jamie McKelvie — DICE Interview

Their discussion of expressive acting shows how art can carry a feeling and free dialogue to contribute something else. Their collaboration involves ongoing decisions about spacing and timing, not a script handed over without discussion.

Read: interview sections on expressions, scripting and collaboration.

14. Kurt Busiek — Astro City #18

The opening’s social conversation and narration supply different perspectives on retirement and belonging. Storyboarding’s later excerpt analysis follows a father’s explanation and his daughter’s withholding and withdrawal. These are examples of developing emotional context, not instructions to import heavy narration.

Read by Comic: opening story pages 1–4. Storyboarding also read story pages 16–21 of the donated script. Not a complete issue or a comparison with finished printed pages.

15. Kelly Sue DeConnick — Bitch Planet #3

A coercive hearing and a family baking scene develop different kinds of pressure through speech, silence and activity. Humor grows out of the situation. The artist letter treats layouts and final dialogue as collaborative work.

Read by Comic: opening story pages 1–5; Storyboarding read the artist letter and story pages 1–6 of the script. Not the complete issue.

16. Andy Diggle — Green Arrow: Year One #1

The sustained rebuke on story page 4 moves from employment obligations toward a personal accusation, using changing framing and a prop. The listener’s evasive reply gains meaning from the speech it follows. That pattern explains why a joke can reveal character after a real exchange.

Read by Comic: opening story pages 1–5 and start of 6. Storyboarding read story pages 2–7 of the fourth-draft script. Not the full issue.

Our draft used preset rounded rectangles, top-aligned text and surplus lower padding. Several long tails compensated for speaker positions that conflicted with reading order. The next proof should shape each text block deliberately, fit its balloon with balanced breathing room, and plan speaking order in the thumbnails. The letter size should remain readable and consistent; balloon dimensions should change to accommodate the speech.

Todd Klein’s placement guide, now read in its accessible text, reinforces reserving lettering space from the script, protecting faces and planning speaking order. It does not prescribe one universal padding measurement. Snug fit still needs space between letters and the outline.

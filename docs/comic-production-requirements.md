# Drain Damage comic production guide

Owner: Comic, coordinating with Art and Storyboarding.

Scope: Every future issue and replacement panel. Established November 14, 2026.

Read this document before scripting, planning layouts, commissioning artwork or reviewing a comic delivery.
The approved requirements below carry forward between issues. Issue-specific choices belong in that issue's
canonical script and visual references. The production requirements and five practices below were approved by Sean
on November 14, 2026. The script-first reference gate and single-guide organization were requested later that day.
Reference pointers describe where to find evidence, not blanket approval of the listed artwork.

## Start here: one guide for every issue

This is the single entry point for Comic, Art and Storyboarding. Keep reusable rules here and the current issue's
script, reference manifest and continuity ledger beside its assets. Links below are helpers and evidence, not a
requirement to reconstruct decisions from chat history. If a current user correction conflicts with an older asset,
the correction wins; preserve the older asset with its status rather than silently treating it as canon.

Comic owns the issue and its final editorial review. Art creates artwork and self-reviews it; Storyboarding supplies
story and character guidance. Agree file ownership before parallel edits. Consult only current Drain Damage agents;
never contact or retrieve retired Engineering1. Do not wake agents merely to acknowledge a handoff. This document
is a project production guide, not a change to any agent's personal settings or permissions.

### Mandatory pre-panel gate: script → inventory → references → production

Before generating story panels or pages for a new issue:

1. Settle the script/brief enough to identify who appears, where scenes occur, and what each character carries,
   holds, uses, sets down, damages or changes. Preserve approved dialogue and distinguish unresolved story choices.
2. Extract a small, explicit inventory of common and recurring objects, especially items Sean and Tony interact
   with. Include appearance, ownership, dimensions relative to characters, construction, grip/contact surfaces,
   attachments, orientation and relevant states. Include locations and recurring supporting characters as needed.
   Do not invent physical dimensions or story facts to fill a gap; mark them as decisions to establish in the proof.
3. Assemble or commission reference/concept artwork FIRST. Reuse suitable approved masters; commission missing
   character angles, equipment views, interaction studies and scene geometry through Art. Rough reference studies
   are permitted in this gate; they are not story panels. Do not start story-panel generation with unresolved
   recurring designs and hope to repair drift afterward.
4. Keep permanent character identity sheets separate from per-issue costumes, style/color, props and settings.
   Character sheets should show front, three-quarter and profile faces, expressions and full-body relative scale.
   Label each image's role: identity, style/color, geometry, costume, prop mechanics or composition. One image may
   serve several roles only when it actually satisfies each. A construction diagram must not redefine the palette.
5. Review the proposed pack for likeness, proportions, practical construction and consistency. Record accepted,
   provisional, rejected and superseded versions, reviewer/date and specific limitations. Obtain Sean's decision
   for unresolved creative choices; an assembled reference pack does not approve its own contents.
6. Lock the accepted reference versions in the issue manifest. Use the relevant approved images directly with
   every panel request; written descriptions and the immediately previous generated panel are not substitutes.
   Compare new faces and props back to the masters, not only neighboring panels. If the script introduces a new
   recurring object later, pause its affected panels and extend the pack before continuing.

Then plan page reading order, scene maps, ledger and dialogue space; review rough composition/mechanics; generate
independent panels; assemble editable lettering; review each panel, page and neighboring pages; deliver only the
accepted versions. Existing accepted artwork is not automatically commissioned for regeneration by this workflow.

### Reference and handoff fields

Use these fields in the existing issue records rather than creating parallel reporting systems:

- **Issue brief:** title/number, scope, script version, owner, approved choices, open decisions and delivery target.
- **Reference manifest:** stable ID, source path/version, role, character/prop/location, approval status and evidence,
  fixed visual traits, relative dimensions, known limitations and superseded references not to reuse.
- **Panel ledger:** stable panel ID, scene/camera, character positions, costumes, prop holder/location/orientation,
  damage/water state, dialogue/SFX, reference IDs, review status and correction boundary.
- **Art request:** panel ID, action and composition, exact reference roles, required balloon space, what may change,
  what must stay fixed, output dimensions and unresolved items. Preserve prompts and original outputs.
- **Acceptance record:** Art self-review, Comic independent visual review, defects/corrections, selected source,
  assembled-page check, text/order/render checks, and actual delivery/publication verification when authorized.

## Character identity and voice desk reference

These are the current reusable rules; issue costumes and visual treatment do not replace identity.

- Use only **Sean** and **Tony**, no surnames, in reader-facing material.
- Sean is bald, shorter and more heavily muscled than Tony. His facial hair is a **chin beard and soul patch, not
  a full goatee, full beard or moustache**. Preserve established eyes, nose, jaw and head shape. Do not let either
  face drift between panels. The precise facial-hair correction was reiterated by Sean on November 14.
- Sean is well-intentioned, big-hearted, eager to help, a bit dumb and easily confused; he is brand new to plumbing.
  His occasional Computer Science/engineering expertise can surprise people in a relevant situation, but does not
  make him a universal secret genius or experienced plumber. Do not invent degrees, employers or personal history.
- Tony is kind, patient, caring and a confident fourth-generation master plumber with decades of experience; he
  tolerates no unnecessary BS. He can be firm, playful and funny, not merely a straight man or omniscient authority.
- Both are tough and capable in a fight. Tony teaches plumbing, not basic courage or physical competence. Neither
  has encountered monster toilets before the opening encounter. Show shared surprise and determination.
- Professional conduct means careful equipment handling and shoe covers, not proof Sean knows the trade.
- Let traits emerge naturally; do not force biographies, technical surprises or a punchline into every scene.
- Normal Sean source costume is orange shirt and blue overalls. Game Easy/Hard-mode costumes and stylized game
  proportions are separate designs; do not import them into a comic without an explicit issue decision.
- BJ is the established AI/server-rack character, in the same illustration style as the comic. A remote radio
  portrait inset does not put him physically in the room. Preserve who is speaking by phone, radio or in person.

Voice authority: [canonical character guidance][character-guidance].
Do not import unrelated game-only mechanics or unresolved campaign lore as comic facts. Preserve approved wording
until its revision is authorized. For creative prose, use varied natural sentences; avoid repetitive paired fragments
such as “Two plumbers. One impossible job.”, formulaic contrasts and generic trailer-copy rhythms. Such fragments
are rare stylistic options, not the default.

## Approved production requirements

### Consistent illustration style

All artwork within an issue must share its established visual treatment: line weight, drawing detail, hatching,
shading, texture and rendering of characters, environments and props. BJ's AI appearances use the same illustrated
style as the surrounding comic. Replacement panels must match the accepted issue, not introduce a new treatment.

### Consistent coloring

Maintain the issue's established palette, saturation, contrast, paper tone and texture across panels and pages.
Lighting may change when the scene calls for it, but this must read as lighting within the same artistic treatment.
Compare replacements beside neighboring panels and across the issue. For issue 1, use the accepted faded vintage
treatment rather than the stronger saturation of rejected replacement panels.

### Correct geometry, scale and physical action

Rigid objects must have credible construction, proportions and perspective. Doors, chairs, tools and fixtures must
not stretch, warp, grow or change their construction between views. Keep their scale consistent relative to people.
Account for perspective and foreshortening; matching objects need not occupy the same number of pixels.

Check hinges, handles, door swing, furniture joints, floor contact and support. Hands must grip actual surfaces;
arms and other body parts must connect naturally. Physical effort must match the action and resistance shown.
An unobstructed door does not require a full-body struggle. A brace must oppose the door's actual opening direction.

For every carried or mounted object, identify what bears its weight and what prevents tipping, sliding or swinging
during ordinary movement. Show credible attachments and enough separated restraint to control the object's rotation.
A tall shaft standing in a shallow hip cup without upper restraint fails this check, even if the strap looks detailed.
Check arm clearance, walking and tool access. Repetition does not make an impractical design acceptable.

Preserve expressive poses, angled views and depth. Geometric accuracy is not a reason to flatten the final camera.

### Generate panels separately and assemble the page

Plan the complete page and reading order first. Generate each panel as an independent illustration, then assemble
the accepted panels into an editable page layout. Do not generate a finished multi-panel page in a single image.
This supersedes earlier allowances for whole-page generation or a hybrid that still generates some complete pages.
A cover or intentional single-panel splash can be one illustration; it is not a multi-panel generation exception.

Write one standalone prompt per panel. Attach the approved model and relevant pose reference for each visible hero
and the issue style sample. Describe what is happening in this picture, where the actors are, and what they touch.
Use the continuity ledger to select visible facts. Keep past correction history out of the scene description.
Start fresh from those references when a rejected drawing has faulty anatomy or construction.

Keep dialogue, balloons, tails, captions and sound effects editable and separate from the generated illustration.
Determine the dialogue and its space needs before final art; do not squeeze text into leftover space afterward.
Retain independent panel assets so a localized correction does not require regenerating an entire page.

### Spatial, object and action continuity

Treat each location as one persistent physical space. Across camera changes, preserve the positions, orientation,
scale and relationships of fixed objects. Two objects opposite one another remain opposite; a visible door, chair
or fixture cannot relocate or disappear simply because the next panel uses a different perspective.

Objects may be outside the frame or hidden behind something when the camera and scene geometry justify it.
Movable objects may change position through a shown or clearly inferable action. Do not invent an unseen move to
explain contradictory artwork. Preserve damage, water, debris and other changed conditions until the story changes them.

Track carried props as held, stowed with visible support, or set down at an established location. Preserve ownership,
number, dimensions and attachment side. Sean's plunger is carried cup-side-up with the handle pointing down.
Use a back-mounted baldric/sheath with credible weight support and spaced shaft restraints. A tool-belt holder is
an approved alternative: the cup sits above a load-bearing holder, the handle hangs down, and a lower guide controls
swing. Validate body clearance and retention before applying either design. The former cup-down hip cradle is rejected.
The empty carrier remains when the plunger is in use.
Do not mirror its side or shorten the tool to fit a crop. Show important transitions, such as leaving a room and
closing its entrance, clearly enough that the reader can follow the action without an explanation outside the comic.

## Previously approved creative and review rules

- **Check acting for emotional continuity.** Storyboarding identifies what each character has just seen or heard,
  what they want now, and the beat that changes their response. Art shows that response through gaze, expression,
  posture, gesture and timing. Comic reads consecutive panels, including across page turns, with the balloons
  covered first and then with the dialogue. Return a panel if the acting contradicts the situation or the line.
  A calm response can fit a veteran under pressure when the drawing makes that choice legible. In issue 1, Sean
  can snap a freely swinging door shut with one hand while watching the approaching creature and calling for Tony.
- Tony and Sean wear matching white, low ankle-height contractor shoe covers indoors after putting them on.
  Covers enclose the toe, heel, sides and sole, with an elastic opening around the ankle/lower boot; they do not
  climb the calf or change into tall boot guards between panels. Preserve boot shape beneath the thin cover,
  plausible folds and consistent cuff placement relative to ankle and trouser hem across poses and camera angles.
  Real products vary; this is the selected comic design, not a universal measurement for every shoe cover.
  Reference: [Trimaco specification][shoe-cover-reference].
- Preserve character likeness, costume and relative proportions. Sean is shorter and more heavily muscled than Tony.
  Both are tough and capable in a fight. Sean's plumbing inexperience does not make him cowardly or physically inept.
  Tony teaches plumbing and trusts Sean in emergencies. Follow the linked character guidance for their voices.
- Write natural, varied dialogue with room for substantial conversations where appropriate. Every panel does not
  need a quip. Preserve approved wording and clearly distinguish phone, radio and in-person speech.
- Size and shape panels for action, emotional importance, dialogue and pacing. Use varied compositions and narrow
  gutters that make a coherent full page. A monster reveal can deserve half a page; silence alone does not imply
  a small panel. Preserve a clear reading order when using angled panels, insets or border-breaking action.
- Draw each speech balloon and its tail as one continuous outline, with no dividing stroke across the tail root.
  Route tails clear of other balloons. Recheck tips against the rendered heads after every crop or layout change.
- Fit balloons snugly around readable dialogue with comfortable padding. Keep tails above the speaker's head,
  pointing clearly toward that speaker without touching or crossing the face. Do not hide important acting or action.
- Use the established standard American comic trim of 6.625 × 10.25 inches for this series unless Sean changes it.
  Verify lettering and the final page at reading size, not only as a large working image.
- Art performs self-review; Comic independently inspects every delivered panel, the assembled page and neighboring
  pages before accepting or showing the result to Sean. Inspect the whole image after a local repair, including
  anatomy, geometry, continuity, color, style and lettering. A prompt or a passing text check is not visual evidence.
- Return visible defects to Art with specific correction instructions and review the replacement again. Do not call
  a known defective panel accepted on the assumption that it will be repaired later. Report unfinished work honestly.
- After incorporating Sean's feedback on a page, show the updated full page once it passes review.
- Preserve original art, rejected candidates, prompts and accepted source assets. Assemble the PDF from accepted
  versions; verify page order, dialogue, rendering and the actual published download before claiming completion.

## Required production practices

Apply all five practices to future issues and relevant new work. Comic coordinates their use with Art and Storyboarding.
These requirements do not call for regenerating existing accepted artwork.

1. **A small reference pack for each issue.** Select an accepted style/color sample, character views with relative
   heights, and recurring prop dimensions before production. Identify the role of each reference so a geometry
   study does not accidentally become the color or character reference.
   Record the accepted style sample's exact file and version as the issue's base image. Give Art that same image
   for every new panel and correction. Compare linework, paper tone, ink density and color against it during review.
   Use character sheets for likeness and scene maps for staging so expressions, lighting and camera angles can vary.
2. **A simple scene map.** For recurring locations, record entrances, door swings, major fixtures and movable props
   in a rough floor plan. Use it to place cameras and check what each view can actually show.
3. **A panel continuity ledger.** Alongside the script, record character positions, prop states, damage and water
   progression. Use stable panel IDs so revisions and delivered files can be matched without guesswork.
4. **An early composition and mechanics check.** Review rough staging, balloon space and complicated physical
   interactions before requesting polished art. If the same defect survives repeated targeted edits, revise the
   blocking or reference instead of repeatedly submitting essentially the same prompt.
5. **An explicit change boundary for corrections.** State what may change and what must stay fixed in each repair.
   Compare the result against the accepted source for collateral changes before replacing that source.

## Practical lessons: script to readable page

This section distills our corrections and craft study into working instructions. Apply it with the requirements
above; the linked research is optional explanation, not homework needed to understand the rules.

### Story, dialogue and a drawable instant

- Establish each scene's objective, what each person knows or wants, and what changes by its end. Draft the whole
  exchange before dividing it into panels. Questions, evasions and admissions must affect what follows.
- Give dialogue substance and varied length. A paragraph can earn its space through character, explanation or
  conflict; do not make every panel a setup and one-liner. Read the conversation aloud in character.
- Describe one drawable instant per panel. A simultaneous punch and nozzle thrust can show teamwork; approaching,
  striking, recoiling and picking up a tool are successive events and need separate beats or a clear omission.
- Match speech duration to action. Put fuller conversations in moments that can plausibly last; do not put a
  leisurely explanation inside a split-second impact. Silence and reactions can carry the scene without dialogue.
- Introduce a device, gauge, clue or destination before dialogue depends on it. Make technical discoveries readable
  to a non-specialist through visible cause and effect. Do not use unexplained jargon as a substitute for a clue.
- Ask whether an inference survives without the author explaining it afterward. A complaint must read as a complaint;
  saying someone was at a desk with shampoo does not establish that they came there to demand running water.
- When a reviewed scene changes, reconcile its downstream payoffs before producing later pages. Track what is
  already shut off, discovered or resolved; do not repeat an earlier solution as a new climax.
- Give setup details a purpose or payoff. Do not spend panels on a mysterious tag or technical procedure merely to
  make the scene sound clever. Distinguish proposed lore from approved game canon and preserve intended ambiguity.
- When the event changes, audit surrounding dialogue and backgrounds: ceremony versus reception, who can hear a
  fight, staff versus guests, deadlines, props and later references. Intercut action and music only with clear timing
  and geography; a cutaway does not repair a missing physical transition.

### Page composition and camera

- Thumbnail the complete page with the actual approved words before polishing panels. Check the facing pages and
  page turn in the assembled book, including cover and indicia placement; do not guess from a story-page number.
- Allocate area according to the important action, acting and dialogue. A major breach can take half a page; a
  close-up can be compact. Vary widths, heights, insets and occasional angled borders when they help the sequence.
  Rectangles remain useful. Irregular shapes are not a quota and must not obscure reading order.
- Make one coherent page with narrow, deliberate gutters and an intentional outer margin. Avoid disconnected
  postage-stamp illustrations, accidental half-empty panels and arbitrary blank bands added by automatic fitting.
- Compose for the panel's actual aspect ratio. If a crop cuts off faces, tool contact or the action being discussed,
  change the layout or request a suitable composition. Do not stretch the image or blindly center-crop it to fit.
- Preserve angled views, depth and expressive staging. Use fixed scene geometry to support the camera, not to force
  every image into a flat front view. Receding hands, limbs and objects must share the scene's perspective.
- Reserve space where the dialogue will actually go, using the speakers' order. If speaker positions fight the
  lettering, adjust the staging at the rough stage rather than compensating with long, crossing tails afterward.

### Lettering and unambiguous reading order

- For this English-language comic, guide the reader left to right within a clear row, then down. A question on the
  right only slightly higher than an answer on the left will often read second. Do not rely on a tiny height offset.
- Sketch the balloon sequence before drawing tails. Place the first line in an obvious entry position; use distinct
  rows when needed. Read the balloons without the script and confirm question → answer → follow-up in a quick glance.
- Keep one consistent readable type scale at final trim. Balance line breaks into a compact text block; size the
  balloon to that block with even breathing room. Do not use huge preset containers or shrink text to hide poor fit.
- Draw balloon and tail as one continuous silhouette and outline. No stroke should close the balloon across the
  tail root. Drawing a triangle and then outlining a complete oval over it creates the rejected seam.
- Identify exactly one speaker per ordinary speech balloon. Aim the tail clearly toward that person's head, stopping
  above it with visible clearance. A tip floating between two people is ambiguous even if it touches neither.
- Tail placement is tied to the final image transform. Recheck it after cropping, resizing or moving the panel.
  Coordinates called `head_top` are not evidence that the real drawing's head is there.
- Avoid tails through faces, hands, important action or other balloons. Do not route a tail behind another speaker's
  balloon so that it appears to emerge from that balloon. Long angular detours are a layout warning, not a solution.
- Join balloons only when they carry the same speaker's continuing thought. A join must be visually intentional;
  do not accidentally connect an answer to somebody else's question. Combining a continuous speech into one balloon
  is acceptable if its wording, rhythm and available space allow it.
- Give BJ a consistent electronic/broadcast treatment and a clear tail to his illustrated AI inset or established
  radio source. Do not make the reader infer the speaker from a label alone. Keep phone speech separately staged.
- Audit every line against the approved script, including small phone lines, interrupted speech, captions and SFX.
  Then inspect the rendered page at reading size; text extraction cannot verify visual order or speaker attribution.

### Physical continuity and efficient corrections

- Follow forces as well as poses. Someone who releases a door before it breaks cannot stumble because its support
  vanished. Show the loaded contact and loss of support, or choose a battle-ready response instead.
- Track a door's hinge side, opening direction, attached knob and threshold. Show how heroes and creature change
  rooms before anyone holds the door closed. Do not repair contradictory movement with an unseen recapture.
- Check the actual joint or pivot of an operated object. A lever on a vertical valve stem rotates around that stem;
  it does not fold upright. Follow the rotation plane and foreshortening through the open and closed views.
- Furniture remains one rigid object when tilted: seat, back and legs move together. Check the real contact points
  before drawing a brace. An attached handle cannot float off its door. Never lengthen a chair or tool to reach.
- Check anatomical left/right, thumb position, palm/back-of-hand orientation, elbow connection and limb length.
  A hand touching a surface must contact it on the correct side. An action reference is useful before repeated edits.
- Continue damage on the same anatomical part through camera changes. Reader-right is not a permanent monster-side
  identifier. Broken teeth, cracks, debris and released equipment must survive into subsequent views.
- Use targeted reference research for the actual uncertainty: shoe-cover height, a valve pivot or a chair's contact
  points. Record the chosen design and practical conclusion here or in the issue reference pack; avoid unnecessary
  scientific analysis when a simple visual comparison answers the question.
- Do not assume prompt length explains a bad result. Check the source, reference roles, requested action and drawing.
  Keep prompts focused on one scene. Repeated edits to a malformed image can preserve its defects; start fresh from
  approved masters and better blocking when necessary. Do not recycle rejected images as identity references.
- References improve consistency but do not guarantee it. Inspect every delivery yourself. Fix a lettering problem
  in the editable lettering layer; do not spend image-generation credits redrawing good artwork to change a tail.

### Review, handoff and release discipline

- Review in three passes: individual anatomy/contacts; sequence geography, damage and acting; then assembled layout,
  reading order, tail attribution and exact copy. Inspect a repaired panel in full, not just the corrected crop.
- Keep status precise: delivered asset, reviewed rough, finished art, assembled proof, approved issue and published
  download are different milestones. Art's self-review does not replace Comic's independent acceptance.
- After feedback, show the corrected full page. Do not leave finished rough deliveries idle without assembly or a
  visible review artifact. Comic owns the next handoff and clearly states anything still awaiting review.
- Build from explicitly selected sources. Check the actual PDF, page count, order and visible revisions before
  publication; then check the public download itself. A new filename, successful upload or old cached link is not proof.
- Keep the cover, inside-cover/indicia and story pages in the intended order. Include the approved JB publisher mark,
  accurate credits and applicable license notices; playful fictional indicia must not invent real publication facts.
- Preserve useful rejected and early art for the making-of picturebook, with its rejected/draft status. Retention
  does not make it eligible for the live comic. Maintain existing records instead of adding another tracking system.

### Maintaining this guide

When a recurring problem is resolved, quietly add or refine its general rule here with a concrete check that would
have caught it. Merge duplicates. Keep transient deliveries, approval dates and outstanding issue tasks in their
existing issue records, not in a growing session transcript. Do not promote an untested suggestion into a requirement.

## Finish and deliver checklist

- [ ] Script, common-item inventory, character identities and issue reference versions are established before panels.
- [ ] Scene maps, stable panel IDs and continuity ledger cover the actual sequence.
- [ ] Comic has checked each character's acting against the preceding beat and the dialogue at reading size.
- [ ] Actual dialogue space and rough physical interactions have passed the early check.
- [ ] Panels are independent; lettering remains editable; no full multi-panel image-generation shortcut.
- [ ] Art self-review and Comic independent review cover identity, style, palette, anatomy, geometry, contact,
      attachments, spatial/action continuity and readable lettering at final size.
- [ ] Local repairs are checked against masters and the entire image for collateral drift. Preserve rejected versions.
- [ ] Updated full pages are shown after feedback, rather than only corrected crops.
- [ ] Order, exact approved dialogue/SFX, rendering, trim and file integrity are checked; status states remaining gaps.
- [ ] Finished issues include PDF, CBZ and genuine RAR-based CBR; never rename a ZIP to CBR. A clearly labeled partial
      proofreading preview may have only the expressly approved formats. Do not claim a final issue from a preview.
- [ ] Publication is separately authorized and verified at the actual destination; preserve applicable MIT notices,
      do not invent real contributors or add “All rights reserved”. Fictional humorous credits remain clearly fictional.
